What your market actually says

The research, consolidated. Their pain points, their objections, and the exact words they use when nobody is selling them anything.

August 11, 2026  ·  One document, as requested

On our last call you said it would be fine if I got the pain points and the objections into one thing. So this is that.

The full research runs to thirteen files, and most of it is working material that only matters to whoever is sitting down to write a page. You don't need that. What you need is the part that tells you whether we're aimed at the right person, and the part you can pull from later when you're making content.

So the shape of this document is simple. It opens with the six things worth knowing, then it goes through what hurts, what they argue back with, what they believe that keeps them frozen, and what they actually want on the other side of it. The last two sections are the useful ones for you specifically: a swipe file of real sentences that real people typed, and the short list of questions only you can answer.

You can read the first box and stop, and you'd still have the important part. The rest will be here when you want it.

Start here

If you read nothing else on this page, read these six.

  1. They are not short on information. They're short on permission and a person. They've watched the videos and can recite the steps back to you. What they cannot do is believe it applies to them, which means any content that just explains the process is competing with ten thousand free videos and losing.
  2. The biggest hole in the category is on the India side, and you're the one person who can fill it. FEMA, ODI, RBI compliance. Every competitor video in this space gets the same angry comment about it. We turned that into a paid-tier qualifier on our call, and it is still the strongest thing the research found.
  3. Your market already has a name for "tolerating." They call it golden handcuffs. They use it about themselves, constantly, and nobody taught it to them. We spent weeks building language for a thing they had already named.
  4. The market described your moat out loud, in public, without being asked. A comment under a competitor's video reads: "You need an Indian guy to explain things simply. You can watch 1000 videos, but one Indian guy will simplify it for you." That is your entire differentiation, requested by name, by a stranger.
  5. Money is the loudest fear in the room, and it isn't close. Not fear of failure, not visa confusion. Cost. And they're being quoted wildly different numbers by wildly different people, so honest pricing sets you apart before you say anything else.
  6. Search will not be our channel, and now we can prove it. The demand is thin and the surrounding keyword space is full of people looking for a job at an American company, not people who want to own one. LinkedIn, your own audience, the community, and the December convention. That's the plan.

The honest partWhere these words came from

Worth thirty seconds, because it changes how much weight to put on any given line.

Roughly half of this is you. Your books, the SHINE outline, your talks, and about six hours of recorded calls where you were describing these people without necessarily knowing that's what you were doing at the time. Twenty-five years of practice, one very experienced point of view.

The other half I went and gathered. I pulled the comment sections underneath the biggest videos in this exact space, the ones with fifty and ninety thousand views on registering a US company from India. I read a hundred and eleven discussion threads where Indian founders talk to each other about this when there's no course being sold. And I pulled the real search data for what your market types into Google.

What this is not

It is not five conversations with people who have sat across from you. Forum comments skew young, male, urban, English-fluent and a little grievance-forward, so they're excellent for words and unreliable for conclusions about culture. Anything in this document that sounds like a cultural claim traces back to something you said, not something I decided.

I'd still like to do the real client interviews. Not in August, because your August is already full. September, before we write the launch emails, while there's still time for what they say to change something.

Section oneThe seven pain points, loudest first

Ranked by how much noise they make in the market, not by how serious they are legally. If we can only speak to a few, we speak to these, in this order.

Pain 01  ·  The loudest one in the room

"How much is this actually going to cost me?"

"Kitna price mein sab complete hoga?" How much will it all cost?  ·  YouTube comment "You saved me 10k because someone was doing same things for 35k." YouTube comment

Money beat everything. It is the first question under almost every video, it comes in three languages, and it comes before anyone asks whether the thing is even legal for them.

What's underneath it is not poverty. It's that they have no idea what the honest number is, and they've been quoted amounts that differ by a factor of three. So the fear isn't the price. It's being taken.

What we do with itPublish real numbers, line by line, including the ones that are annoying. Honest pricing is a differentiator in this category all on its own, which is a strange thing to be able to say.

Pain 02  ·  The one your whole platform answers

"Is this even possible for somebody like me?"

"Most of us Indians have been taking a 'safe, secure route' out and not taken a RISK at all." Reddit thread "It seems too complicated to achieve, too grand a plan, too large a mountain to climb." You, Chapter I

This is the master pain and every other one is this one wearing a different outfit. They don't doubt that Indians can own American companies. They doubt that they can, and the doubt is so old it doesn't feel like a doubt anymore. It feels like a fact about the world, right?

Your line from the book is still the best description anyone has written of what this feels like in the body: a tightness in the throat or the chest, and the thing gets put on the back shelf for a later date. But you certainly dream about it.

What we do with itNothing on the page argues with them. Recognition is what converts a reader like this, so we describe the situation with uncomfortable precision and let them do the arguing.

Pain 03

"Where do I even begin?"

"Non US founder here, how do you even start a US company. I did not expect the amount of paperwork, weird requirements and confusing state rules." Reddit "Still undecided between a C-Corp or LLC, and also unsure about the best state." Reddit

Decision paralysis at step one, and it's worse than normal paralysis because the internet gives them a confident and contradictory answer every time they look. Delaware or Wyoming. LLC or C-Corp. One person says you need a passport, the next says you don't.

The experience they describe is overwhelm, not fear. Different feeling, different fix.

What we do with itSequence beats information. Six steps, in order, in plain English, with the reasoning attached so the order feels obvious instead of arbitrary.

Pain 04  ·  Corrected by you on 8-07

"Will Americans take me seriously from over here?"

"The market was big, but credibility was missing." Reddit "How can you call it your own company? You're working from the other side. This isn't a company, this is like a job." YouTube comment, translated

I originally wrote this up as bias, and you corrected me on the call. The barrier is visa economics, not prejudice. Sponsorship now costs an employer six figures, so the employer does the math and moves on, and that is a fact rather than an accusation.

Your correction made the argument better, not just safer. Bias is something a person endures. An economic barrier is something they route around, and routing around it is exactly what your offer does.

What we do with itThe US entity, the bank account and the trademark are the instruments that answer it. And your own line closes the loop: you don't need to be there to make that money.

Pain 05  ·  Rising fast, owned by nobody

"Nobody is telling me about the India side."

"You are not talking about FEMA and ODI compliance that one has to do in INDIA." YouTube comment "Don't give half-baked information. Learn the FEMA law first before doing something like this, otherwise RBI will levy a heavy fine." YouTube comment, translated from Hindi "I am tired of the ODI process." r/StartUpIndia, thread title

An Indian resident who owns a foreign company is making an overseas investment, and that pulls in a whole second country's worth of obligation. Everyone teaches the American half. Almost nobody handles both ends, and the market is furious about it.

On our call this stopped being a content gap and became something better. The people who feel this pain have already formed something, already spent money, and already hit a wall, which makes them the most qualified buyers in the market. The pain sorts them for us.

What we do with itName it, don't teach it. You said it plainly: you can explain it but you find it confusing, so nothing on this subject gets written by us. Your sourced material or nothing at all.

Pain 06

"What if I build something real and somebody takes it?"

Quieter in the forums than the first five, and much louder once someone has actually started. It arrives late, it arrives suddenly, and it usually arrives because something already happened to them or to somebody they know.

This is Module 3's front door, and it's also the sharpest trigger in the whole list, because a person who just found their name on someone else's website is not in a research mood. They want to talk to someone this week.

What we do with itThe IP checklist magnet is aimed here. Hook: they are already worth copying, which is a compliment and a warning in the same sentence.

Pain 07

"I have a full-time job."

"No one quits their job to start something when they have serious responsibilities like taking care of old family member, sick person, new born." Reddit

Practical on the surface and something heavier underneath. They're not saying they lack hours. They're saying the hours they have are already promised to people who are counting on them.

What we do with itShort lessons built for a phone on a commute, and nothing expires. Also the reason the whole model works: you don't have to leave, so nothing anyone depends on has to change.

Section threeWhat they believe that keeps them still

Six beliefs. Every one of them was installed to keep this person safe, and most of them worked.

A language rule we're holding to

We call these protective beliefs, never limiting beliefs, in anything a customer will ever see. Telling an accomplished adult that the thing which got them here was a defect is a bad way to open a relationship. Internally the other word is fine. On a page it never appears.

They think"People like me, from where I am, can't."

Where from
Generations of it. This is the one you've been describing to us since May.
Actually true
Immigration status is not the gate. Someone who has never held a green card can form and own a US entity.
What it costs
The whole dream, indefinitely, at no visible price. Which is exactly why it survives.

They think"I need to be certain before I start."

Where from
The perfection thing you named, plus a school system where one exam decided the rest of your life.
Actually true
The first step is reversible, cheap, and takes days.
What it costs
Two years of research and no filing. The founder who has been "looking into incorporation" since 2024 is not lazy and not scared. They're waiting to be certain, and certainty isn't coming.

They think"If I fail at this, everyone will know, and it will cost my family."

Where from
Achievement is family currency, and it gets spent in public.
Actually true
Forming a company is private. Nothing about it needs announcing until it works.
What it costs
They keep it secret, which removes all their support, which makes failure likelier. The secrecy causes the thing it was protecting against.

They think"Americans won't take me seriously from here."

Where from
Visa economics, per your correction. Six figures to sponsor, so the employer never gets to the part where they'd find out how good someone is.
Actually true
The presence is the instrument that answers it. That's what Module 2 is for.
What it costs
They compete as a vendor instead of as a company, and they price themselves accordingly.

They think"This costs more than I have."

Where from
Currency math, contradictory quotes, and some genuinely predatory pricing that is out there right now.
Actually true
No minimum capital exists, and the real costs are knowable and much smaller than the fear.
What it costs
They never even price it out. The fear does the deciding.

They think"Wanting more than this is ungrateful."

Where from
Parents who sacrificed for the exact outcome this person is now sitting inside of.
Actually true
I don't know yet, and this is a question for you. If it's real, it may be the deepest blocker in the whole list, and SHINE's "soul-aligned" is already the answer to it.
What it costs
It turns ambition into betrayal, which is not a thing a person can argue themselves out of.

Section fourSix things they don't know they don't know

A belief is something they'd tell you. A blind spot is something they'd be surprised by. This is where content earns trust fastest, because telling a person who thinks they've read everything something they hadn't heard is the quickest route to credibility there is.

  • They think the hard part is America. The hard part is India. They research US formation obsessively and find out about FEMA and ODI after they've already formed something. Biggest finding in the whole harvest.
  • They don't know what they signed when they were hired. The side project may legally belong to their employer, and nobody in this category is telling them that.
  • They think "no personal US tax" means no tax work. The myths tell them what they don't owe. Nothing tells them what the company itself has to file, and the comments are full of people finding out at the one-year mark.
  • They think the obstacle is knowledge. It's sequence, judgment, and having someone to ask the follow-up question. So they'll keep buying information and stay exactly where they are, which is a sad thing to watch happen at scale.
  • They think going quiet was free. Your line names the bill they've been paying without noticing: carrying feelings you had to subdue in order to get to the next stage.
  • They think they need to be less Indian to compete in America. SHINE's "H" is the direct answer, and the market is asking for the opposite of what they believe. They're in comment sections asking for an Indian person to explain it to them.

Why this section matters more than the pain section

The pain points tell us what to say on a sales page. The blind spots tell us what to make content about for the next year, because each one is a video where you tell a smart person something they didn't know they didn't know. You said the research would be useful for creating content later. This is the part I'd start with.

Section fiveWhat they actually want

You can't write to fear responsibly unless you know exactly where you're landing them afterward, and "stay positive" is not a plan. Four rungs, and your offer is unusual because it credibly reaches all four.

01

"I want the thing to exist."

A company with their name on it, an EIN, a bank account that works, an address, a trademark, an invoice that gets paid in dollars. Your Chapter I does this beautifully by naming the pieces instead of describing the dream. The wish for income in a currency that doesn't shrink is real and shouldn't be sanitized. It's arithmetic against a ceiling they can already see.

02

"I want to be seen differently."

Your own phrase for it is the secret envy of people who already do business in America, alongside the quiet conviction that you're as good as they are. There's a family version of this too, and it's the one we handle with care: at weddings, "my son is studying at IIT" becomes "my son owns a company in America." That isn't vanity. In this culture it's how a family says out loud that the sacrifice paid off.

03

"I want to be someone who builds."

This rung is where your moat is, and it's yours in your own words: that part of you that's been waiting, sometimes silently, to feel permission. Not to do more, but to be more. The shift is from someone who complies excellently to someone who builds. Everything in their history has rewarded the first thing and nothing has ever rewarded the second.

04

"I want this to have meant something."

Being the first in the family to own something rather than earn from something. Children growing up around someone who built. Your mango tree, and sharing the fruit instead of hoarding it. Use it sparingly and never first, because opening here reads as fluff to an engineer. Ending here lands.

The balance note I care most about

Their life is good. Salary, respect, family, a career their parents can name proudly at a party. Any copy that implies their life is a failure gets rejected instantly, and worse than that, it feels like it was written by somebody who doesn't know them.

The gap isn't between misery and happiness. It's between fine and theirs, and that's the whole emotional territory we're writing inside. It's one word wide.

Section sixWhere you corrected me

Both of these came out of the walkthrough on the 7th, both are now folded into the research, and both made the copy better rather than just safer.

"I don't think it's a bias. I think it's a genuine immigration situation. Getting a visa for your employee Indian is harder than getting it for maybe your British or your French employee. There's a new law suddenly that the employer has to pay over $100,000 just to sponsor it. So naturally, the employer is going to be like, why." Megha, August 7

I had this written up as prejudice, based on threads where Indian applicants described getting filtered out immediately. You reframed it as economics and you were right on all three counts that matter.

It's safer, because a claim that American companies are prejudiced is a claim under your name as a licensed New York attorney with no way to source it, while a sponsorship cost is a fact. It's a better argument, because bias is something a person endures and an economic barrier is something they route around. And it survives the protective-belief framing instead of undercutting it, since now there's a real mechanism sitting underneath the fear.

The second one was smaller and the same instinct. On the comment about walking away from a stable job and making "some family excuse," I read it as hiding the venture from family. You read it properly. The excuse is being made to the employer. It's resignation cover, not family secrecy, and it would have been miscited in copy if you hadn't caught it.

Why I'm putting this in a client document

Because it's the whole model working, right? I bring the raw finding rather than the polished version, you correct the part only twenty-five years in the practice would catch, and the output is something neither of us would have written alone. Both corrections happened specifically because I showed you the messy version. So I'm going to keep doing that, and you should keep red-penning it.

Section eightWhat I still need from you

Four things, and one of them is worth more than everything else on this page. Voice notes are completely fine and honestly faster for me than written replies.

Ask one  ·  highest value in the document

What had just happened, the week they finally called you?

We agreed not to use fake urgency on this audience. No countdown timers, no seats running out, none of that machinery, because it reads as a scam to them and it isn't you. Which means the only honest way to get somebody to move now is to reach them in the week that something already happened.

So think about the last ten clients who came to you about setting up in America. Not why they wanted it. What happened right before they picked up the phone. A layoff round. A promotion that landed flat. A US client asking whether they were a US entity. Somebody copying their name. A birthday with a zero in it. A parent getting sick.

Why I'm pushing on this oneTen answers here would be worth more than another month of me doing desk research, and it would rewrite the entire email sequence.

Ask two  ·  blocking about 24 pieces of content

Is SHINE for women, or for everyone?

Your own solution statement still has the bracket in it where you asked yourself this question, and you've already written a version that isn't gendered. Your practice is majority male entrepreneurs, and when you tested SHINE live it was the men in the room who responded hardest. Then on the 7th you said something that pushed the other way, about not wanting the women to feel the program is aimed at men.

What I'd doMake the framework and the marketing gender-neutral, and leave the stories exactly as they are. When you tell the steakhouse mirror story you tell it as a woman who lived it, and a man reading it doesn't need it softened. He needs it true.

Ask three

There's a section of this research about why your market goes quiet. Every bit of it traces back to something you said. But you said it on a call, not for publication, and that difference matters.

CJ and I are two American guys writing about this at scale with your name on the finished product. So I'd rather hand it to you and be told where it's wrong than find out later from someone in your community.
